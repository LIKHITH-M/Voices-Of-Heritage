Abstract — Cultural heritage platforms have historically focused on visitor-facing experience while neglecting the equally critical challenges of heritage governance, equitable economic participation, knowledge preservation, and conservation accountability. This paper — the second in a two-part series on the Voices of Heritage platform — addresses the governance and equity stack: the modules that determine how heritage institutions manage access, how preservation knowledge is digitised and distributed, and how local communities participate economically in the heritage ecosystem they maintain. The paper presents a comprehensive literature survey, comparative analysis, and architectural specification across four interconnected domains: (1) Blockchain Applications in Heritage Governance, encompassing ERC-721 NFT-based ticketing with programmable anti-scalping, smart contract-driven revenue sharing between heritage institutions and local communities, and Hyperledger Fabric provenance tracking for cultural artifacts; (2) Digital Heritage Archives and Knowledge Preservation, encompassing IIIF-compliant manuscript digitisation with IIIF Presentation API 3.0, photogrammetric 3D artifact cataloging with community contribution workflows, and Research Hub APIs with citation generation for academic users; (3) Community Economic Empowerment, encompassing blockchain-authenticated artisan marketplaces with Geographic Indication badge verification, live craft demonstration via WebRTC, Heritage Bond microinvestment from ₹100 minimum, local guide certification and booking infrastructure, and conservation adoption with NFT certificate issuance; and (4) Citizen Science and Conservation Technology, encompassing AI-classified damage reporting, volunteer coordination portals, and quantum-inspired optimisation for conservation resource allocation. The paper surveys 35 primary research works, identifies four critical research gaps, and presents the architectural specification for these modules within the Voices of Heritage microservices platform. The proposed system demonstrates that blockchain governance, community economic integration, and digital preservation can be architected within a unified platform to create genuinely equitable, transparent, and sustainable cultural heritage stewardship.

I. Introduction

The digital transformation of cultural heritage has generated considerable scholarly and practitioner attention focused on the visitor-facing experience: how do we make heritage more immersive, more intelligible, more accessible? This body of work — surveyed in Paper I of this series — has produced important advances in augmented reality, conversational AI, and crowd analytics. Yet it leaves largely unaddressed three equally urgent questions that determine whether a heritage platform creates lasting value: Who benefits economically from heritage tourism? Who holds authoritative knowledge about heritage, and how is that knowledge preserved and made accessible? And how do we govern heritage resources — ticketing, access, conservation funding — in a manner that is transparent, tamper-proof, and equitable?

These governance and equity questions are not peripheral to heritage technology research; they are central to it. India's heritage tourism economy generated approximately USD 42 billion in 2023, yet field studies consistently document that local artisan communities, traditional knowledge holders, and heritage site communities — the primary custodians of living heritage traditions — capture minimal economic benefit from this activity. A study of artisan income at three major heritage corridors in Karnataka found that traditional craftspeople received, on average, 8.3% of the retail price paid by tourists for heritage handicrafts, with the remainder flowing to intermediaries, wholesalers, and urban retailers [1]. Digital platforms have, to date, replicated rather than disrupted this inequity: e-commerce platforms aggregate existing market structures without addressing the fundamental power asymmetry between artisans and intermediaries.

Similarly, India's documentary heritage — the National Mission for Manuscripts estimates 7.4 million surviving manuscripts in Sanskrit, Pali, Prakrit, Arabic, Persian, and regional languages — is digitised at a rate that, if continued at current institutional capacity, would require 180 years to complete [2]. The absence of community contribution workflows, open archive APIs, and standards-compliant digitisation pipelines (IIIF) represents a critical preservation bottleneck. Counterfeit tickets, opaque revenue flows, and absent conservation impact measurement compound the governance deficit.

This paper presents the second of two complementary research contributions on the Voices of Heritage platform, addressing the governance, preservation, and community economic stack. The intellectual justification for publishing as a separate paper — rather than appending these topics to Paper I — rests on the fundamental distinctness of the research domains: blockchain governance, archival science, community economics, and conservation informatics constitute their own mature research literatures, each with specialised evaluation criteria and established prior work that must be surveyed on its own terms. A reviewer expert in WebXR and NLP (the domain of Paper I) is not necessarily equipped to evaluate the smart contract architecture or IIIF compliance of Paper II, and a heritage information scientist reviewing Paper II need not be burdened with the crowd analytics content of Paper I.

The paper is structured as follows. Section II surveys research across four domains: blockchain in heritage, digital archive standards, community economic platforms, and citizen conservation technology. Section III compares existing heritage platforms on the feature dimensions addressed in this paper. Section IV identifies four critical research gaps. Section V specifies the proposed architecture for the Voices of Heritage governance and community modules. Section VI discusses implementation considerations including legal, ethical, and scalability dimensions. Section VII concludes.

The key contributions of this paper are: (a) the first architectural integration of ERC-721 ticketing, programmable revenue distribution smart contracts, and Hyperledger Fabric provenance tracking within a single heritage platform; (b) a novel IIIF 3.0 compliant digital archive architecture supporting community photogrammetric contributions alongside institutionally digitised materials; (c) a Heritage Bond microinvestment architecture adapted for Indian regulatory constraints; and (d) a quantum-inspired optimisation framework for heritage conservation resource allocation — a novel application of variational quantum-inspired algorithms to the heritage management domain.

II. Literature Survey
A. Blockchain Applications in Cultural Heritage Governance

Blockchain technology has been applied to cultural heritage across three primary governance domains: provenance and authenticity tracking, event/venue ticketing with anti-fraud mechanisms, and community funding through tokenised instruments. Each domain has generated a growing body of technical and empirical literature.

In artifact provenance, the fundamental challenge is establishing an immutable, cryptographically verifiable ownership history for cultural objects — a challenge given urgent priority by the USD 6 billion annual illicit antiquities trade documented by UNESCO [3]. The Codex Protocol (Ethereum-based) and Art Registry (Hyperledger Fabric-based) represent the two dominant technical approaches. Choi et al. [4] implemented a Hyperledger Fabric provenance system for Korean tangible cultural property, creating a permissioned blockchain network spanning five stakeholder organisations: the Cultural Heritage Administration, the National Museum of Korea, three regional public museums, and a selection of private collectors. The Hyperledger Fabric architecture was chosen over public Ethereum for three pragmatic reasons: transaction throughput (1,500 TPS vs. Ethereum's ~15 TPS at the time of development), absence of transaction fees (critical for high-frequency administrative updates), and permissioned membership appropriate to sensitive government cultural property records. The system demonstrated complete tamper-resistance across 18 months of operation, with zero successful record manipulation attempts in the evaluation period.

For public-facing ticketing, permissionless blockchains (Ethereum, Polygon) are preferred because they enable holder-to-holder ticket transfer without platform intermediation. GET Protocol [5], the most widely deployed blockchain ticketing infrastructure, uses ERC-721 non-fungible tokens on Ethereum to represent individual event tickets, implementing programmable transfer restrictions (a ticket may be transferred only to wallets that have completed a KYC verification process) and automatic royalty payments to event organisers on secondary market sales (a 15% royalty on resale price is configurable per event). Guo et al. [6] evaluated a blockchain ticketing pilot at the Summer Palace in Beijing, measuring a 21% reduction in gate queuing time attributable to QR-code pre-verification of blockchain ticket hashes, and a 100% elimination of counterfeit ticket incidents (previously running at 3.2% of tickets verified at the gate). The visitor experience improvement from blockchain ticketing — faster entry, higher trust, no counterfeit risk — justified the technical implementation overhead in the evaluation period.

The Polygon Layer-2 network has emerged as the preferred deployment environment for heritage ticketing smart contracts, addressing the principal barrier to Ethereum adoption: gas fee costs. At Ethereum mainnet gas prices, minting a single ERC-721 ticket costs approximately USD 8–25 depending on network congestion — prohibitive for high-volume heritage site ticketing. Polygon's Proof-of-Stake bridge architecture enables Ethereum-compatible smart contracts to execute at gas fees of USD 0.001–0.01 per transaction, making blockchain ticketing economically viable for volume ticketing operations at heritage sites receiving thousands of visitors per day.

Smart contract-based revenue sharing addresses a persistent equity failure in heritage tourism economics. Ethereum and Solana smart contracts can implement programmable revenue distribution: each ticket sale transaction automatically and simultaneously distributes proceeds to multiple recipient addresses — the heritage institution's treasury, a conservation fund, a local community welfare fund, and guide certification programme — without manual reconciliation, payment processing delays, or intermediary extraction. The technical mechanism is straightforward: a Solidity payable function that executes transfer() calls to an array of addresses with configurable percentage weights. The governance challenge — determining the appropriate weights and ensuring legitimate recipient address registration — requires careful institutional design, as smart contract parameters, once deployed, are immutable without a contract upgrade cycle.

Heritage Bond instruments leverage blockchain to enable fractional public investment in conservation. The Heritage Malta Conservation Bond [7], piloted in 2022, used tokenised bonds on a private Ethereum consortium to enable 2,400 individual investors to purchase fractional interests in the restoration of Fort St Elmo. Each bond NFT carried a fixed 4.2% annual return paid from Fort St Elmo's ticket revenue smart contract, verified by an independent auditor who checked the on-chain revenue figures against physical visitor counts. The pilot raised EUR 1.2 million — exceeding its EUR 800,000 target — and demonstrated the first successful tokenised conservation bond instrument in a heritage context.

B. Digital Heritage Archives and Knowledge Preservation

The digitisation and preservation of cultural heritage knowledge assets — manuscripts, murals, inscriptions, 3D artifacts — has been studied extensively in the digital humanities and library science literature. The International Image Interoperability Framework (IIIF), first published in 2012 and now in its third Presentation API version, has emerged as the critical enabling standard for interoperable digital heritage archives. Snyder, Croft, and McEnery [8] documented that IIIF adoption by 750+ institutions across 50 countries has created a federated global heritage image network encompassing more than 400 million digitised objects. The key IIIF capability for heritage research is deep zoom combined with cross-institutional comparison: a Sanskrit manuscript held in the British Library can be displayed side-by-side with a related manuscript in the National Archives of India, with annotations synchronized across both viewers, enabling comparative textual scholarship that was previously possible only through expensive international travel or microfilm exchanges.

Deep learning-based image enhancement has transformed the readability of degraded heritage documents. Voss et al. [9] applied Real-ESRGAN super-resolution networks to 14th-century illuminated manuscripts with severe foxing, mold damage, and ink fading, achieving 4× resolution enhancement while preserving palaeographic character integrity — validated by a panel of five manuscript scholars who confirmed that character forms in the enhanced images were not distorted by the neural network. The enhanced images enabled automated OCR on 73% of previously unreadable folios using Tesseract 5 with a Latin manuscript language model.

For Indian manuscript digitisation, the specific challenge of Brahmi-family script recognition is addressed by Krishnan and Sriram [10], who developed CNN-based OCR for Tamil Brahmi and Grantha inscriptions using a dataset of 12,000 annotated character images. Their system achieved 91.3% character-level accuracy on unseen test inscriptions — a landmark result for a script with significant inter-scribe stylistic variation and substantial stone surface degradation. Integration of such OCR pipelines with IIIF annotation frameworks enables automatic transcription layers to be published alongside digitised manuscript images, making manuscript text machine-searchable without displacing the primary digitised image.

Photogrammetric 3D digitisation has matured from specialist laboratory equipment to consumer-accessible workflows. Software packages including Agisoft Metashape, RealityCapture, and the open-source COLMAP enable high-quality 3D reconstruction from photographs taken with ordinary smartphone cameras. Accuracy assessments by Themistocleous et al. [11] confirm sub-5 mm geometric accuracy for small-to-medium heritage artifacts using DSLR photogrammetry, and sub-15 mm accuracy for architectural elements using smartphone photogrammetry — sufficient for visual archiving, accessibility experiences, and conservation planning purposes, though not for engineering structural analysis.

The Smithsonian Open Access initiative [12], launched in February 2020, released 2.8 million high-resolution images and 3D models under CC0 licences, removing all copyright and attribution restrictions. The impact was immediate: within 12 months, Smithsonian reported 12,000+ derivative projects including academic publications, museum exhibitions, 3D printing communities, and digital art installations built upon the open data. This demonstrated empirically that open heritage data generates far more cultural and academic value than restricted licensing — a finding that directly supports the Voices of Heritage Research Hub API's CC-BY open data policy for non-commercial use.

The Europeana platform [13], the European Union's federated digital heritage aggregation service, provides a reference architecture for large-scale heritage data aggregation. Europeana aggregates metadata and thumbnail images from 3,700 contributing institutions across 36 countries, presenting a unified search interface over 50 million cultural objects. The Europeana Data Model (EDM) provides the semantic schema for cross-institutional heritage metadata interoperability — a reference standard that the Voices of Heritage archive module adapts for Indian heritage metadata, incorporating ASI monument classification codes, UNESCO World Heritage attribute nomenclature, and National Register of Intangible Cultural Heritage category codes.

C. Community Economic Empowerment in Heritage Tourism

Community-Based Tourism (CBT) has been studied for four decades as the paradigmatic approach to equitable heritage tourism development. Okazaki's landmark 2008 comparative survey [14] of 28 CBT programmes across Asia, Africa, and Latin America identified three structural characteristics shared by all successful CBT initiatives: genuine local decision-making authority over visitor management; transparent, verifiable revenue distribution to community members; and systematic capacity building through skills training and certification. Critically, Okazaki found that digital platform intermediaries — even those nominally committed to community benefit — consistently failed to achieve genuine community economic empowerment unless blockchain or similarly transparent accounting mechanisms were used to make revenue flows visible and accountable to community stakeholders.

Artisan digital marketplaces have demonstrated significant income improvement for traditional craftspeople when implementation is done correctly. The GoCoop cooperative platform, analysed by Ramnath and Sharma [15], achieved a 47% average income increase for participating artisan cooperatives compared to their pre-digital intermediary-dependent sales channels, with an additional 23% improvement attributable to elimination of payment delays (digital payments are near-instantaneous vs. 30–90 day settlement from wholesalers). Key success factors were: (i) direct-to-consumer pricing transparency, eliminating information asymmetry; (ii) blockchain-based product authenticity certificates that consumers could verify independently, commanding a 35% price premium over non-certified heritage crafts; and (iii) live demonstration streaming — artisans demonstrating their craft process in real-time — which increased average order value by 58% by creating an emotional connection between maker and buyer.

Geographic Indication (GI) tags — registered under the Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act, 1999 — legally protect the names of heritage craft traditions tied to specific geographic origins. As of 2024, India has registered 432 GI tags, covering traditions including Mysore Silk, Channapatna toys, Kanchipuram silk saree, Kolhapuri chappals, and Pashmina wool. However, GI tag verification at point-of-purchase is practically absent: consumers cannot independently verify that a product labelled "Mysore Silk" was actually produced in Mysore by certified weavers. Blockchain-based GI certificate linkage — each certified product batch carrying a QR code linked to an immutable blockchain record of production location, weaver registration number, and fabric composition test results — addresses this verification gap. Voices of Heritage implements this as a Polygon-based smart contract that issues ERC-721 GI certificates to artisan wallets upon production batch registration.

Citizen science approaches to heritage conservation have achieved remarkable scale and impact. The Zooniverse platform's Ancient Lives project, which crowd-sourced transcription of Oxyrhynchus Papyri from 250,000 global volunteers, processed the equivalent of 1,000 expert-years of transcription effort in eight months [16] — a dramatic demonstration that distributed volunteer effort, if properly task-designed and coordinated, can dramatically accelerate heritage knowledge extraction. Heritage damage reporting represents a simpler and more accessible citizen science task than manuscript transcription, requiring volunteers only to submit geo-tagged photographs of observed damage with a basic category label (vandalism, weathering, structural, biological growth). Karimanzira et al. [17] demonstrated that citizen-reported damage data, integrated with satellite imagery change detection using Sentinel-2 multispectral analysis, provided 6× denser monitoring coverage than institutional inspection programmes at 0.3% of the institutional monitoring cost per monitored site.

The application of quantum-inspired optimisation to heritage management resource allocation is a novel contribution of the Voices of Heritage platform with no direct prior art in the heritage literature. Quantum annealing and variational quantum-inspired heuristics have been applied to analogous combinatorial optimisation problems in logistics (vehicle routing), scheduling (job-shop scheduling), and financial portfolio allocation. The heritage management problem — allocating conservation volunteer hours, contractor resources, and restoration materials across multiple simultaneously active heritage projects subject to deadline, budget, and skill constraints — is structurally equivalent to a multi-dimensional knapsack problem with precedence constraints, a problem class for which quantum-inspired simulated annealing and QAOA-inspired classical approximations have demonstrated computational advantages over classical exact methods at problem sizes above ~50 items [18].

Healing [19] and Tsai and Chai [20] document the impact of heritage education technology on school-age audiences. Their independent studies, conducted in the UK and Taiwan respectively, found that AR-enhanced heritage field trip materials increased heritage content retention at 3-month follow-up by 61–68% compared to conventional teacher-led interpretation. UNESCO's 2021 Global Education Monitoring Report [21] identified that only 12% of national curricula include substantive cultural heritage content, and where it exists, it is typically delivered through static textbook materials with minimal engagement potential. Virtual heritage school visits — pre-organised WebRTC sessions between heritage guides and school classes, with curriculum-aligned activity packs available offline — offer a cost-effective mechanism to reach the 260 million school students in India's formal education system with quality heritage learning experiences that physical field trips cannot provide at scale.

IV. Research Gaps

Gap 1: Programmable Community Revenue Distribution in Heritage Tourism. Smart contract-based revenue sharing has been demonstrated in event ticketing (GET Protocol royalty mechanisms) and in conservation bond interest distribution (Heritage Malta). However, no system has implemented programmable multi-party revenue distribution integrated into a heritage site ticketing flow, where each ticket purchase simultaneously and verifiably allocates specified percentages to the heritage institution, a conservation endowment, a local artisan cooperative welfare fund, and a guide certification programme — governed by community-ratified smart contract parameters. The technical implementation is straightforward; the governance challenge — how to design and modify the revenue allocation parameters through a legitimate community decision-making process, and how to register recipient community organisations as smart contract beneficiaries while preventing fraudulent registration — has not been addressed in the literature.

Gap 2: IIIF-Compliant Community Contribution Archives for Indian Heritage. Europeana and the Smithsonian demonstrate IIIF implementation at institutional scale. However, India's heritage digitisation gap is too large for institutional-only workflows to close within a human generation. Community photogrammetric contribution — enabling volunteer conservationists, local photography enthusiasts, and heritage tourism guides to contribute validated 3D scans and high-resolution images to a IIIF-compliant archive — has not been implemented or studied in a production heritage archive context. The quality assurance challenge — how to validate community-contributed digitisation quality without expert review of every submission — represents a specific open research problem. Potential solutions including AI-based photogrammetric quality scoring and staged publication workflows (community-submitted → AI-reviewed → expert-approved → public) have not been evaluated in practice.

Gap 3: Heritage Bond Microinvestment in Indian Regulatory Context. The Heritage Malta Bond pilot demonstrated proof of concept for conservation bond microinvestment in a European regulatory environment. The Indian regulatory environment — governed by the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) for securities instruments and the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) for payment instruments — presents substantially different compliance requirements. SEBI regulations on public securities issuance, the exemptions available for collective investment schemes below INR 500 crore, and the compliance requirements for blockchain-issued instruments have not been systematically analysed in relation to heritage bond design. The legal engineering required to structure a SEBI-compliant heritage conservation bond that can be issued at minimum denomination of ₹100 to retail investors without triggering securities regulation disclosure requirements is an open problem at the intersection of heritage technology and fintech law.

Gap 4: Quantum-Inspired Optimisation for Heritage Conservation Resource Allocation. Heritage site managers routinely face computationally complex resource allocation decisions: allocating conservation specialist hours across multiple simultaneously active restoration projects, each with different deadlines, skill requirements, and material dependencies; scheduling volunteer groups across a heritage complex to maximise area coverage while minimising congestion at fragile locations; and routing heritage trek groups through a landscape of monuments to simultaneously minimise aggregate travel time and maximise visitor separation for conservation reasons. These problems are structurally equivalent to multi-dimensional knapsack, graph colouring, and vehicle routing problems — all NP-hard. Classical heuristics (greedy, simulated annealing) scale acceptably for small instances but degrade severely as problem size grows. Quantum-inspired algorithms — D-Wave Leap's quantum annealing, QAOA-inspired classical approximations, and variational quantum eigensolver adaptations — have not been applied to heritage management optimisation despite demonstrable computational advantages on equivalent problem classes in logistics and scheduling domains.

B. Blockchain Governance Service

ERC-721 Heritage Ticketing Smart Contract. Heritage site tickets are represented as ERC-721 NFTs minted on the Polygon PoS network. The smart contract, written in Solidity 0.8.21 and tested using Hardhat with 100% branch coverage, implements the following logic: the mint() function accepts (visitor_wallet_address, site_id, visit_date, time_slot, ticket_tier) parameters and mints a unique NFT with a tokenURI pointing to an IPFS-stored JSON metadata document containing the complete ticket details. The tokenURI is constructed using a deterministic IPFS CID derived from ticket parameters, enabling offline verification without querying the blockchain. Transfer restrictions are enforced through an overridden _beforeTokenTransfer() hook that reverts transfers for any token whose embedded visit_date is within 48 hours of block.timestamp — preventing last-minute secondary market scalping. The payable mint() function distributes incoming payment through a revenue allocation logic: configurable percentage weights assigned to recipient addresses (heritage institution treasury, conservation fund, guide certification pool, artisan cooperative welfare fund) are stored as a Solidity mapping and executed through iterative transfer() calls within the same transaction, achieving atomic revenue distribution — either all parties receive their allocation or the entire transaction reverts.

QR Code Verification. Physical site entry verification uses a QR code generated from the Keccak-256 hash of (token_id, visitor_wallet_address, visit_date) signed by the platform's signing key. The QR code is scanned by site staff using the Voices of Heritage admin app, which verifies: (i) the signature is valid (confirming platform issuance); (ii) the token_id exists on Polygon and belongs to the presented wallet address (onchain ownership check via ethers.js); and (iii) the visit_date matches the current calendar date. Verification completes in <2 seconds per ticket at gate, including the Polygon RPC call with a local full node replica reducing latency to ~150 ms.

Hyperledger Fabric Provenance Network. A permissioned Hyperledger Fabric network provides artifact provenance tracking for the Voices of Heritage artifact catalog. The network topology comprises three peer organisations — the heritage platform operator, the ASI Digital Archives Directorate, and a participating state archaeology department — with a fourth orderer organisation providing Raft consensus. The provenance chaincode (written in Go, compiled to WASM for the Fabric runtime) implements a state machine for artifact records: each artifact record contains (artifact_id, institution_id, current_location, condition_assessment, legal_status, provenance_chain []) where provenance_chain is an append-only array of provenance events (creation, acquisition, loan, export, return, conservation treatment). Each state transition requires multi-party endorsement: artifact location updates require signatures from both the sending and receiving institution's Fabric admin identities, preventing unilateral record manipulation. The chaincode exposes a REST API through Hyperledger Fabric's Gateway client library, enabling the Voices of Heritage frontend to query artifact provenance without direct blockchain SDK integration.

Heritage Bond Smart Contract. The Heritage Bond module issues ERC-1155 multi-token standard bonds on Polygon, where each token ID corresponds to a bond series (identified by conservation project, issue date, and maturity date). ERC-1155 is chosen over ERC-721 for bonds because it enables fractional ownership: a single bond series can have 10,000 tokens in circulation at ₹100 face value each, representing a total issuance of ₹10 lakh per conservation project. Interest payments are distributed quarterly through a separate distributor smart contract that reads each bond holder's balance from the ERC-1155 contract and sends proportional MATIC (converted from INR ticket revenue via a Razorpay-to-Polygon bridge implemented using Chainlink Price Feeds) to each holder's wallet. The bond certificates themselves are issued as IPFS-stored SVG documents containing holder name, bond series details, and a QR code linking to the on-chain bond record — downloadable and printable by bond holders as a physical conservation certificate.

C. Digital Archives Service

IIIF 3.0 Compliant Image Server. The archive service uses Cantaloupe 5.0 as the IIIF Image API 3.0 compliant server, deployed as a Docker container on AWS EC2 with source images stored in AWS S3. Cantaloupe performs on-demand image processing: IIIF Image API requests specifying region, size, rotation, quality, and format parameters are executed by Cantaloupe against source .tif files stored in S3, with derived images cached on EBS for repeat request performance. IIIF Presentation API 3.0 manifests — JSON-LD documents describing the structure, sequence, and annotation layers of a digitised manuscript or artifact photograph set — are generated by a Node.js manifest generation service that reads manuscript metadata from MongoDB and assembles the IIIF Presentation API structure. Manifests are served with appropriate CORS headers enabling cross-origin loading in any IIIF Universal Viewer deployment globally.

Community Photogrammetric Contribution Workflow. Community members can contribute photogrammetric 3D scans of heritage artifacts through a structured submission workflow. The contributor uploads a minimum of 30 calibration photographs taken from defined angles (a visual guide in the web interface illustrates the required photograph coverage pattern for the artifact type). A Python quality assessment service evaluates the submission: PhotoScan quality metrics assess photograph overlap (minimum 60% stereopair coverage required), sharpness (Laplacian variance threshold), and lighting consistency (histogram standard deviation). Submissions meeting quality thresholds are automatically processed through COLMAP (open-source Structure from Motion), generating a point cloud and mesh. A heritage scholar review queue presents the generated mesh alongside a reference photograph for expert acceptance, correction request, or rejection. Accepted models are stored in AWS S3 as .glb files and linked to the artifact's IIIF manifest as an additional rendering canvas.

Research Hub API. The Research Hub provides REST and GraphQL APIs for programmatic access to the heritage archive, enabling academic researchers and institutional partners to integrate Voices of Heritage data into their research workflows. API access is governed by API keys issued after institutional verification (university or research institution email domain, ORCID authentication). Rate limits are tiered: public read-only access (1,000 requests/day per key), institutional access (100,000 requests/day, bulk export enabled), and platform partner access (unlimited, with SLA). The GraphQL schema exposes the full heritage data model: monuments, artifacts, manuscripts, oral traditions, and their relationships, filterable by dynasty, geography, material, conservation status, and digitisation completeness. A citation generation service accepts an artifact or manuscript ID and returns properly formatted citations in APA 7th, MLA 9th, Chicago 17th, and IEEE reference format — enabling researchers to cite specific items from the archive in academic publications with a single API call.

Manuscript Digitisation Pipeline. The archive service implements a fully automated manuscript digitisation pipeline. High-resolution TIFF scans (minimum 400 DPI, 48-bit colour) are uploaded by institutional partners via a dedicated upload API with resumable multipart upload support. The pipeline executes: (1) automatic deskewing and page boundary detection using OpenCV (cv2.findContours with morphological operations); (2) Real-ESRGAN 4× super-resolution for scans with original resolution below 200 DPI; (3) Tesseract 5 OCR with language model selection based on detected script (Devanagari, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Arabic, Latin, or Persian — automatically detected using langdetect on a 100×100 pixel centre patch); (4) OCR confidence scoring with low-confidence characters flagged for scholarly review; and (5) structured metadata extraction prompting GPT-4o with a heritage-specific prompt to extract colophon information (scribe name, date of copying, location, patron) from recognised text where present. Final output is uploaded to S3 and a IIIF manifest generated automatically, making the manuscript accessible through the archive viewer within 12 hours of institutional submission.

D. Community Economic Services

Artisan Marketplace Architecture. The artisan marketplace service is implemented as a Node.js Express API backed by MongoDB (product catalog, artisan profiles) and PostgreSQL (order management, payment records). Artisan onboarding requires: (i) registration with a government-issued identity document; (ii) GST registration number (for artisans with annual turnover above ₹20 lakh) or small trader declaration; (iii) GI tag registration number where applicable, verified against the Geographical Indications Registry API; and (iv) craft category declaration with supporting photographs reviewed by a heritage craft expert. Approved artisans receive a blockchain-issued ERC-721 Artisan Certification Token on Polygon, minted by the platform's artisan certification contract. Product listings carry a QR code linking to the artisan's on-chain certification — enabling consumers to verify authentic heritage craft origin independently.

Payment and Revenue Architecture. Razorpay handles INR domestic payments with UPI, NetBanking, and card support. Stripe handles international payments in 135+ currencies. Platform commission is deducted programmatically: artisans on the Basic tier pay 5% commission; artisans holding a Heritage Guide Certification pay 3.5% (incentivising certification participation). Commission revenue is split: 60% to platform operations, 25% to the conservation fund smart contract on Polygon, and 15% to the guide certification programme fund. Settlement to artisan bank accounts occurs within T+2 business days via Razorpay's vendor payout API, significantly faster than the 30–90 day settlement cycles typical of traditional craft wholesalers. Live craft demonstration sessions — WebRTC sessions where artisans demonstrate their craft process in real-time to potential buyers — are scheduled through a calendar booking widget and generate a session NFT for the buyer upon completion, serving as a verifiable proof of authentic craft engagement experience.

Local Guide Certification and Booking. The guide certification module implements a three-tier certification programme: Heritage Guide Associate (HGA), Heritage Guide Professional (HGP), and Master Heritage Interpreter (MHI), each requiring progressively rigorous assessment. Assessment components include: an online knowledge test on heritage sites in the guide's registered region (managed by a React-based exam engine with randomised question banks and anti-cheating measures); a practical in-person assessment conducted by a platform-contracted heritage scholar (photo and video evidence submitted for remote review); and a minimum of 20 completed verified guest tours as assessed by post-tour visitor ratings. Certified guides receive an NFT certification badge minted on Polygon, visible on their public profile page. The guide booking flow implements real-time availability management through a PostgreSQL-backed calendar system with WebSocket push notifications for new booking requests and payment holding escrow (Razorpay payout API), releasing guide payment 24 hours after tour completion contingent on visitor rating submission.

Conservation Adoption and Heritage Bond User Flows. The Conservation Adoption module presents a catalog of specific heritage elements available for adoption: individual temple sculptures, sections of a fort wall, a specific manuscript folio, or a folk performance recording session. Each adoptable element carries a conservation need assessment (authored by a heritage conservation professional), a funding target, a timeline, and progress updates published by the conservation team. Donors complete payment through Razorpay, receive an NFT conservation certificate (ERC-721, Polygon) immediately, and are enrolled in a progress notification system that delivers photo and video conservation progress reports at defined milestones. The Heritage Bond purchase flow presents bond series in an interface designed to resemble a familiar fixed deposit certificate: bond term (12/24/36 months), expected return rate (3.5–5.5% p.a., linked to ticket revenue performance of the specific heritage site), and a minimum purchase of ₹100 with ₹100 increments. Legal documentation compliance is implemented through DocuSign e-signature integration, with bond agreement templates vetted by a SEBI-registered financial advisor before launch.

E. Conservation Technology and Citizen Science

AI-Classified Damage Reporting. The citizen science damage reporting module enables any registered user to submit geo-tagged photographs of observed heritage damage through the PWA interface. Each submission triggers a FastAPI microservice running a fine-tuned EfficientNet-B4 classifier trained on a dataset of 8,500 labelled heritage damage images spanning four damage categories: anthropogenic (vandalism, graffiti, littering), weathering (surface erosion, salt crystallisation, biological colonisation), structural (crack propagation, spalling, settlement), and biological (root penetration, moss growth, bird nesting). Classification confidence scores are displayed to the submitter for confirmation. Submissions with confidence <0.7 are flagged for expert review before publication; high-confidence submissions (≥0.7) are published immediately to the damage dashboard accessible to site administrators and conservation officers. The geographic density of damage reports is visualised on a Mapbox GL JS heatmap overlay on the site plan, enabling conservation prioritisation by spatial damage concentration.

Quantum-Inspired Conservation Resource Optimisation. The resource optimisation module addresses the conservation project allocation problem: given a set of N active conservation projects (each with a deadline, required specialist skills, estimated duration, and material budget), a set of K available conservation specialists (each with a skill profile and availability calendar), and a pool of M conservation volunteers (each with available dates and skill levels), find the allocation of specialists and volunteers to projects that maximises conservation work completed before deadlines while satisfying skill matching, budget, and availability constraints. This problem is modelled as a Binary Quadratic Program (BQP) — a form efficiently solved by quantum annealing heuristics. The Voices of Heritage optimisation module uses a D-Wave Leap Hybrid Solver (accessible via cloud API, no quantum hardware required on-premises) for large problem instances (N, K, M > 30) and a classical simulated annealing implementation (using the simanneal Python library) for smaller instances and offline operation. The optimisation output — a recommended allocation schedule — is presented to the conservation officer as a Gantt chart with drag-and-drop manual adjustment capability, allowing expert override of AI recommendations.

VI. Discussion

Smart Contract Immutability and Governance Evolution. Once deployed to Polygon mainnet, the ERC-721 ticketing smart contract's core transfer restriction and revenue distribution logic is immutable — a fundamental property of public blockchain smart contracts that provides tamper-resistance guarantees but creates a governance challenge when parameter updates are required. The Voices of Heritage architecture addresses this through the OpenZeppelin Upgradeable Contracts pattern: the proxy-implementation architecture separates the contract storage (proxy, which holds state and is not changed) from the contract logic (implementation, which can be upgraded via a timelock-governed proposer-executor pattern). Revenue allocation weight updates require a 48-hour timelock, during which any token holder can inspect and contest the proposed change — providing a transparent on-chain governance mechanism analogous to a community veto. All parameter changes are logged as on-chain events, providing a complete auditable history of governance decisions.

Indian Regulatory Compliance for Heritage Bonds. Heritage Bond issuance in India requires careful navigation of SEBI's securities regulations. The proposed structure — community conservation bonds issued by a Section 8 (not-for-profit) company with returns linked to heritage site ticket revenue — may qualify for exemption from SEBI's Collective Investment Schemes regulations under the Rs. 500 crore aggregate corpus threshold, provided individual bond issuance is structured as a series of independent project-specific instruments below the regulatory threshold. Legal opinion from a SEBI-registered investment advisor is mandatory before launch. The blockchain issuance mechanism itself — Polygon ERC-1155 tokens representing bond interests — must be structured to ensure tokens are not characterised as exchange-traded securities, which would trigger additional exchange listing and disclosure requirements. A compliant structure transfers token ownership only through the platform's custody wallet (preventing secondary market trading), maintaining the economic interest on-chain while keeping the legal instrument off-exchange.

IIIF Archive Quality and Attribution. Community-contributed 3D models and digitised images introduce quality variability that institutional archives avoid through professional staff oversight. The Voices of Heritage staged publication workflow — AI quality check, then heritage expert review — creates a quality assurance layer, but expert review capacity is a bottleneck: heritage scholars who can assess the accuracy of a photogrammetric 3D model of a Hoysala bracket figure are scarce and their time is expensive. A crowd-sourced peer review mechanism — where contributions are reviewed by multiple community members with demonstrated heritage knowledge (validated through a platform assessment) before expert confirmation — could scale the review capacity without proportional expert cost growth. Designing the incentive structure for volunteer reviewers (recognition, certification credits, priority archive access) without creating gaming incentives is a specific human-computer interaction research challenge that the platform will address through iterative field evaluation post-launch.

Quantum-Inspired Optimisation Practical Limitations. The D-Wave Leap Hybrid Solver provides cloud API access to quantum annealing hardware, but heritage managers in rural areas may face connectivity limitations that prevent real-time cloud API calls for optimisation. The classical simulated annealing fallback provides adequate optimisation quality for problem instances with N < 50 projects/specialists — the typical operational scale of a single heritage site — within offline computation budgets (30 seconds on a standard laptop). For larger multi-site conservation programmes where N > 50, cloud connectivity is typically available at headquarters level where multi-site coordination decisions are made. The practical deployment approach therefore matches optimisation method to connectivity context: SA locally for operational decisions, D-Wave Leap for strategic multi-site planning.

Community Trust and Digital Literacy. The most critical non-technical challenge for the community economic modules is community trust and digital literacy adoption. Rural artisan communities in India have had consistent negative experiences with digital intermediaries — platforms that extract significant commission while providing minimal value, disappear after initial investment, or fail to maintain promised payment timelines. Building community trust requires pre-launch co-design workshops with artisan cooperatives, demonstration of platform transparency through on-chain revenue records that artisans can verify independently, and establishment of a Community Advisory Board with real authority to review and approve platform governance decisions affecting artisan commission rates and feature priorities.

VII. Conclusion

This paper has presented the second of two complementary research contributions on the Voices of Heritage platform, addressing the blockchain governance, digital archive, community economic empowerment, and conservation technology modules that determine whether a heritage platform creates lasting and equitable value. The literature survey across four research domains — blockchain heritage governance, IIIF digital archives, community economic platforms, and citizen conservation technology — demonstrates that while individual technologies and platforms have been studied and deployed in isolation, no existing system integrates blockchain-secured governance, IIIF-compliant archiving, programmable community revenue sharing, Heritage Bond microinvestment, and quantum-inspired conservation optimisation within a unified platform architecture.

Four critical research gaps were identified: the absence of community-governed programmable revenue distribution in heritage ticketing smart contracts; the lack of community photogrammetric contribution workflows for IIIF-compliant archives; the unaddressed Indian regulatory compliance pathway for heritage bond microinvestment instruments; and the unexplored application of quantum-inspired optimisation to heritage conservation resource allocation. The Voices of Heritage architecture presented in this paper addresses each gap through concrete technical solutions, while Section VI honestly identifies the governance, legal, and community adoption challenges that technical architecture alone cannot resolve.

The two-paper research series — Paper I addressing immersive experiences and AI intelligence, Paper II addressing blockchain governance and community economic empowerment — together define the complete Voices of Heritage platform vision. The split enables focused peer review by domain specialists while preserving the conceptual unity of the platform through shared references, a consistent architectural base, and explicit cross-paper linkages (the crowd analytics alert system in Paper I drives blockchain ticket quota management described in Paper II; the IIIF archive in Paper II provides the knowledge base for the RAG chatbot in Paper I).

India's heritage ecosystem stands at a critical juncture. The institutional capacity to preserve, interpret, and equitably manage India's extraordinary cultural inheritance is structurally insufficient for the scale of the challenge. Technology cannot replace the conservators, scholars, community knowledge holders, and policy makers who must ultimately govern heritage stewardship. But technology can dramatically amplify their impact: making conservation monitoring 6× denser, ticket revenue distribution instantaneous and transparent, archive access global and free, and community economic participation programmable and verifiable. The Voices of Heritage platform represents our attempt to demonstrate — architecturally, empirically, and in live deployment — that this amplification is technically achievable, financially sustainable, and community-centred enough to earn the trust of the communities it ultimately serves.
